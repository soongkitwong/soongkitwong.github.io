About

Research at the intersection of information, identity, and choice.

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Agricultural and Applied Economics at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. My research studies how identity changes the way people interpret and use social information when outcomes are uncertain.

My work sits at the intersection of behavioral economics, identity economics, experimental economics, and policy analysis. I am especially interested in when observed choices reveal stable preferences and when they instead reflect differences in information, trust, perceived similarity, or consideration.

Job Market Paper

Identity as Information: Perceived Risk-Preference Similarity and Informational Influence in a Laboratory Experiment on Decisions under Risk

Behavioral economics; identity economics; experimental economics; risky choice

In many consequential decisions, people must commit to an option before they can fully evaluate how it will perform for them. Other people's choices can help fill this information gap, but only if those people are seen as relevant counterfactuals. This paper tests whether shared identity affects how people use others' choices when payoff information is hidden, preventing direct evaluation of the options. In an incentivized experiment, 226 participants choose between lotteries, first independently and then after observing the previously recorded choices of other participants (referents). Across repeated blocks, I vary whether participants share the referent's gender and whether the lottery probabilities and possible payoffs are visible.

Participants are more likely to copy the referent when these details are hidden, with a larger increase for same-gender referents. This gender pattern is consistent with a perceived-similarity mechanism. Random assignment to a same-gender referent increases perceived similarity in risk preference before any referent choice is revealed, and perceived similarity predicts copying when lottery details are hidden. Together, the gender-matching and perceived-similarity results point to a role for shared identity in informational influence: it shapes whose choices people treat as informative.
