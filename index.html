About

Research at the intersection of information, identity, and choice.

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Agricultural and Applied Economics at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. My research studies how identity changes the way people interpret and use social information when outcomes are uncertain.

My work sits at the intersection of behavioral economics, identity economics, experimental economics, and policy analysis. I am especially interested in when observed choices reveal stable preferences and when they instead reflect differences in information, trust, perceived similarity, or consideration.

Job Market Paper

Identity as Information: The Impact of Identity-Mediated Information in a Risk Choice Experiment

Behavioral economics; identity economics; experimental economics; risky choice

This paper studies how identity shapes social learning under uncertainty. In a preregistered lab experiment, subjects first make baseline lottery choices that reveal their risk preferences. They then complete repeated choice blocks after observing other subjects, referred to as referents. Referents are experimentally assigned to vary gender match, while payoff visibility is also randomized: in payoff-visible trials, subjects can evaluate the lotteries directly; in payoff-blind trials, the referent's choice becomes the main available signal.

Before choosing, subjects report how closely they think the referent's preferences align with their own. Same-gender referents are perceived as closer in preferences, and perceived closeness predicts copying especially when payoff information is hidden. The results show how identity can affect learning through perceived informational relevance, especially when information is scarce.
